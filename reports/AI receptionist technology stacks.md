# Keep the engine, buy headroom, measure latency

*Research report, dated 2026-10-13.*

AI phone receptionists almost never build their own speech technology, and the ones winning the market do not compete on it. The few confirmed disclosures share one shape. Yelp puts OpenAI's full-duplex GPT-Live-1 in front of its own service-area, calendar and routing logic. ConverseNow licenses Rime's text-to-speech and routes work across several LLMs. Platforms like Synthflow rent OpenAI, Deepgram, ElevenLabs and Twilio. Avoca, Slang, Hostie, Loman, Jobber and ServiceTitan name no vendors at all. What they sell is "trained on millions of calls" plus deep writes into ServiceTitan, POS systems and reservation books. The voice-AI platforms underneath (Vapi, Retell, Bland, Synthflow, ElevenLabs Agents, Twilio ConversationRelay) are cascaded speech-to-text → LLM → text-to-speech orchestrators. They charge a **$0.05–0.09/min platform fee** on top of the components, so realistic all-in costs run **$0.10–0.25/min**. Speech-to-speech models (OpenAI Realtime and GPT-Live-1, Gemini 3.8 Live, Ultravox) cost more again in production and carry reported tool-calling regressions. Our stack uses Twilio Media Streams, Deepgram Flux with built-in end-of-turn detection, Groq-hosted gpt-oss, Deepgram Aura-2, and a rules engine that owns every decision. That matches the best-documented architecture in the field, where the LLM proposes and code disposes, and it is stricter than Pipecat Flows or Retell's flow agents. It also costs an **estimated $0.031–0.034 per call-minute**, about 3–7x less than a hosted platform. Its weaknesses are operational, not architectural. Nobody has measured its voice-to-voice latency on a real phone line, and its tests never touch audio. Both LLM layers sit idle, because Groq's free tier (reported at 8K tokens/minute) cannot carry even one live call at typical prompt sizes. And nothing audits every call automatically. The recommendation is to keep the architecture and every vendor, and change things in this order. First, add per-turn timing and an audio replay test now, which costs nothing. Second, switch on Groq's paid tier and the turn reader the day the first customer pays, for an estimated $1–2 per customer per month in tokens. Third, run a Spanish speech-to-text and text-to-speech bake-off on real 8 kHz calls. Leave the LLM voice layer for last. The targets are **p50 ≤ 900 ms and p95 ≤ 1.5 s caller-perceived latency**, **≤ $0.04/min in English and ≤ $0.05/min in Spanish**, and **≤ $25/month in AI and telephony costs per $199 customer**.

*How to read this report: every number about our own system is an **estimate**. Each one comes from list prices, third-party benchmarks and the system description, because nothing about latency or cost per minute has been measured yet. "Vendor claim" marks numbers a company publishes about itself. The research environment could not open most vendor pages, so many figures come from search-result summaries of the cited pages, and several third-party pricing blogs contradict each other. Check live pricing pages before committing a budget.*

## Vertical leaders hide their vendors and compete on business logic

**Only three products on the list have confirmed what they run on, and none of them is a small receptionist app.** Yelp's September 2026 announcement says OpenAI's GPT-Live-1 is now "the front-end voice layer that interacts directly with callers" in Yelp Host and Hatch, on top of "a proprietary multi-provider voice AI stack" ([Yelp press release](https://www.yelp-press.com/press-releases/press-release-details/2026/Yelp-and-Hatch-Advance-Voice-AI-for-Restaurants-and-Service-Pros-with-OpenAIs-GPT-Live-1/default.aspx)). ## Platforms rent the same cascade and charge a per-minute toll

The voice-AI platform market splits three ways. The first group is **hosted cascaded orchestrators**: Vapi, Retell, Bland, Synthflow, ElevenLabs Agents, Telnyx, Deepgram's Voice Agent API, Cartesia Line and Twilio ConversationRelay. The second is **open-source frameworks** with optional hosting: LiveKit Agents and Pipecat. The third is **native speech-to-speech APIs**: OpenAI gpt-realtime-2.x and GPT-Live-1, Gemini 3.8 Live, and Ultravox. Vapi runs endpointing, interruption detection and backchanneling on its own infrastructure and plugs in Deepgram, AssemblyAI, Gladia and other speech-to-text providers, plus ElevenLabs, Cartesia, Deepgram and other voices ([Vapi data flow](https://docs.vapi.ai/security-and-privacy/data-flow)). Synthflow runs Deepgram self-hosted on Google Cloud, with Nova-3 or Flux for speech-to-text and Aura-2 for text-to-speech ([Deepgram customer story](https://deepgram.com/customers/synthflow-ai)). Bland claims it hosts its own ASR, inference and TTS in one deployment ([Bland](https://www.bland.ai/)). Most platforms accept your own LLM endpoint. The surfaces closest to a custom stack are the "LLM-only" ones, which handle audio, speech-to-text, text-to-speech and turn-taking while the conversation logic stays on your server. **Twilio ConversationRelay** sends text prompts to your WebSocket and now supports Deepgram Flux ([Twilio changelog](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/changelog/conversation-relay-now-supports-deepgram-flux---new-features)). Retell's custom-LLM WebSocket works the same way, though Retell's own docs advise using it only when compliance requires it, because built-in agents get features first ([Retell](https://docs.retellai.com/integrate-llm/overview)).

Pricing makes the case against migrating. A lean receptionist's total component cost is close to the platform fee alone.

**Platform risk is not hypothetical.** Vapi retired Workflows in August 2026 ([Vapi migration](https://docs.vapi.ai/workflows/legacy-migration)). It logged 16 incidents in 90 days, including a 22-hour number-provisioning outage in September ([StatusGator](https://statusgator.com/services/vapi)). Reviewers report 2–5 s real turn gaps against what its dashboard shows ([Quiq](https://quiq.com/blog/vapi-ai-review/)). Cartesia stops hosting Line agents on 2026-12-01 ([Cartesia changelog](https://docs.cartesia.ai/changelog/2026)). OpenAI deprecated its first gpt-realtime model within about a year ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-realtime)). Retell's simulation testing, a key selling point, does not support custom-LLM agents ([Retell docs](https://docs.retellai.com/test/llm-simulation-testing)). Our engine would therefore lose that feature on Retell anyway. The best argument for a platform is a solo founder's time: turn-taking, barge-in, backchannel filtering, tracing and simulation come built in. But our system already has the hard parts (Flux end-of-turn, a deterministic engine, scenario tests). The remaining gaps cost less to build than a migration, which would also add $0.05–0.09/min.

**Speech-to-speech is the architecture to watch, not to adopt.** OpenAI's gpt-realtime-2.1 costs $32/$64 per million audio input/output tokens, and the mini costs $10/$20 ([OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)). Nominal estimates of about $0.02/min for the mini compare with a **measured $0.063–0.146/min across 4,000 production sessions**, because context is billed again on every turn ([HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/openai-realtime-api-pricing-in-2026-real-world-data-from-4000-measured-sessions)). Gemini 3.8 Live lists about $0.005/min in and $0.018/min out, but Google's forum confirms the same re-billing ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/pricing-of-speech-to-speech-live-model/140340)). It also has no native telephony and caps audio sessions at 15 minutes ([Google](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/live-api/start-manage-session)). Developers report that gpt-realtime-2.1 answered instead of calling tools in about 25% of attempts in one unverified test ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/gpt-realtime-2-1-production-tool-invocation-regression-model-answers-instead-of-calling-available-tools/1398438)). Others say speakerphone noise makes the model talk over callers and mishear names and addresses ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/realtime-api-poor-portuguese-call-quality-with-gpt-realtime-mini-gpt-realtime/1381375)). GPT-Live-1 is different and worth tracking. It is a full-duplex front end that hands reasoning and tool calls to a backend model, priced at **$0.05/min plus the backend** ([OpenAI GPT-Live-1](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-live-1)). This is Yelp's design, and our engine is exactly the kind of backend it is built to call.

## Component by component, our stack sits on the field's defaults

|---|---|---|---|

| Speech-to-text | Deepgram Flux en / multi | Deepgram (Synthflow, Vapi default, ConversationRelay), AssemblyAI, Soniox, ElevenLabs Scribe | Keep; bake off Spanish |
| Turn detection | Flux EOT 0.7 / timeout 3000 ms / eager 0.6 | Flux, LiveKit detector, Smart Turn, OpenAI semantic_vad, proprietary (Retell, ElevenLabs) | Keep; tune per state |
| LLM | Groq gpt-oss-120b / 20b (free tier, layers off); Ollama fallback | GPT-4.1 family default on Retell; OpenAI and others; multi-LLM at ConverseNow | Keep model; move to paid tier |
| Control | Rules state machine; LLM reads only; validated rewording | Prompt + flows (Retell, Bland, Pipecat Flows); Rasa CALM split | Ahead of field |
| Text-to-speech | Aura-2 thalia-en / selena-es, cache; Orpheus option | ElevenLabs, Cartesia, Rime, Deepgram, Inworld | Keep Aura-2; test alternatives for quality |
| Architecture | Cascaded | Cascaded almost everywhere; speech-to-speech at Yelp | Keep |

**Speech-to-text: Flux earns its price through turn detection, not accuracy.** On Coval's daily-rerun board, Soniox stt-rt-v5 (about 48–63 ms to first segment), Baseten's Qwen3-ASR, ElevenLabs Scribe v2 Realtime (4.4% WER) and Speechmatics lead streaming speech-to-text ([Coval](https://benchmarks.coval.ai/models/universal-streaming)). Pipecat's reproducible English test puts Soniox at 1.34% semantic WER against Deepgram Nova-3 at 1.71% ([Soniox citing Pipecat](https://soniox.com/compare-stt/soniox-vs-deepgram)). Soniox costs about **$0.002/min** ([Soniox pricing](https://soniox.com/pricing)), against Flux's **$0.0065/min pay-as-you-go English ($0.0077 list) and $0.0078 multilingual** ([markaicode](https://markaicode.com/pricing/deepgram-pricing/)). Flux bundles end-of-turn detection: Deepgram claims about 260 ms detection and 30% fewer false interruptions than speech-to-text plus VAD ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/learn/introducing-flux-conversational-speech-recognition)). Switching to Soniox would save about $0.005/min but would mean rebuilding turn detection. That is a bad trade at 450 minutes per customer per month. For Spanish, the only head-to-head is a Soniox-run test on wideband audio (Spanish WER 5.3% vs Deepgram's 10.1%), so it says little about 8 kHz phone calls ([Soniox](https://soniox.com/compare/soniox-vs-deepgram/spanish)). Flux multilingual covers 10 languages with code-switching ([Retell STT roundup](https://www.retellai.com/blog/best-speech-to-text-models)). Deepgram's February 2026 Nova-3 multilingual update claims its largest gains in code-switching ([Deepgram changelog](https://developers.deepgram.com/changelog/2026/2/5)). No source benchmarks Spanglish on real mu-law audio, so we have to collect that evidence ourselves.

**Turn detection: our settings are sensible, but the eager setting currently buys little.** Flux defaults to `eot_threshold` 0.7 and `eot_timeout_ms` 5000 ([Flux API reference](https://developers.deepgram.com/reference/speech-to-text/listen-flux)). Our 3000 ms timeout ends idle turns two seconds sooner. Deepgram says EagerEndOfTurn arrives **150–250 ms early when `eager_eot_threshold` is 0.3–0.5**, at the cost of 50–70% more LLM calls that get thrown away on TurnResumed ([Deepgram eager EOT guide](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/flux/voice-agent-eager-eot)). At 0.6, just below the 0.7 final threshold, the early signal will come only slightly ahead of the final one. It saves time only if something speculative starts on it: a reader call, or rendering the likely next line. Flux can be reconfigured mid-stream ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/learn/flux-on-the-fly-configuration)), so per-state tuning is cheap. AssemblyAI's documentation explains why it matters: short silence settings split numbers and proper nouns ([AssemblyAI](https://assemblyai.com/docs/streaming/universal-streaming/turn-detection)). A Hacker News builder found turn-taking, not transcription accuracy, was the real UX problem ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47224295)). If Flux ever needs a second opinion, Pipecat's Smart Turn v3 is open source and runs in about 12 ms on a CPU, with 90.1% Spanish accuracy in v3.1 ([Daily](https://www.daily.co/blog/improved-accuracy-in-smart-turn-v3-1/)). LiveKit's turn detector also lists Spanish ([LiveKit](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/turn-detector/)). Their published comparisons conflict, with LiveKit-run and independent studies favouring LiveKit ([LiveKit EOT bench](https://livekit.com/benchmarks/eot-bench); [arXiv 2609.20995](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.20995)).

**LLM: the model is right, the tier is the problem.** Groq lists gpt-oss-120b at **$0.15/$0.60 per million input/output tokens**, with cached input at half price, and gpt-oss-20b at $0.075/$0.30. Cached tokens do not count against rate limits ([CloudZero](https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/groq-pricing/)). Throughput is about 480–500 tokens/s. Time to first token depends mostly on `reasoning_effort`: 4.87 s to the first answer token at high reasoning, against a reported p95 of 0.50 s ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/gpt-oss-120b/providers); [benchlm](https://benchlm.ai/models/gpt-oss-120b)). The model supports JSON-schema mode and tool use on Groq ([GroqDocs](https://console.groq.com/docs/model/openai/gpt-oss-120b)), which fits the turn reader. Third-party sources report the free tier at **30 requests/min, 1,000/day and 8,000 tokens/min** on gpt-oss-120b, and the Developer tier at 1,000 requests/min and 250,000 tokens/min ([CloudZero](https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/groq-pricing/); [klymentiev](https://klymentiev.com/blog/groq-pricing)). Cerebras serves the same model at about 3x the throughput for $0.35/$0.75 ([morphllm](https://www.morphllm.com/cerebras-pricing)). That makes it the natural same-model failover. Closed "mini" models sit around 0.8 s time to first token ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/gemini-2-5-flash-preview-09-2025-reasoning-vs-gpt-4-1-mini)), and reasoning models are unusable for voice unless thinking is switched off. Practitioners name minimal thinking as one of the biggest latency wins on receptionist turns ([sharvinshah.com](https://www.sharvinshah.com/guides/voice-ai-agents)).

**Text-to-speech: Aura-2 is adequate and Spanish-capable, but it is not a naturalness leader.** Artificial Analysis's September 2026 boards put **Eleven v4/v4 Turbo, Cartesia Sonic 3.6 and Inworld TTS-2** at the top. Inworld costs about $20.8 per million characters, ElevenLabs $40–80 and Cartesia $49 ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/text-to-speech/leaderboard/controlled-voice)). Aura-2 costs $0.030 per thousand characters (about $30 per million) ([aiagentsquare](https://aiagentsquare.com/agents/deepgram)), so Inworld is both cheaper and ranked higher. Its telephony output format and latency were not verified. Groq's Orpheus costs $22 per million characters but is **English and Arabic only, in preview, capped at 200 characters per request**, and limited on the free tier to 100 requests/day ([GroqDocs](https://console.groq.com/docs/text-to-speech/orpheus)). It is not a production path for a bilingual product. Independent time-to-first-audio p50s run 155–340 ms (Cartesia 188 ms, ElevenLabs Turbo 264 ms, Rime 337 ms with high variance) ([Gradium citing Coval](https://gradium.ai/content/tts-latency-benchmark-2026)). Vendor claims of 40–90 ms measure the model alone. No independent figure exists for Aura-2. ConverseNow's double-digit gain in call success after a voice swap is the strongest evidence that **voice quality changes outcomes**. That makes text-to-speech the component most worth A/B testing once there is call volume, especially the Spanish voice.

## Latency is unmeasured, and the engine is our biggest lever

The practitioner target is **about 800 ms median voice-to-voice, with 1.5 s acceptable for early prototypes** ([Kwindla Kramer, Pipecat](https://gist.github.com/kwindla/f755284ef2b14730e1075c2ac803edcf)). Callers start talking over the agent at about 300 ms past that ([husnainbukhari](https://www.husnainbukhari.com/blog/self-host-ai-voice-agent)), and calls "audibly degrade" around 1.2 s ([HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/the-voice-agent-latency-playbook-stt-turn-detection-and-the-tradeoffs-nobody-talks-about)). Published platform numbers disagree because their definitions disagree. Twilio reports p50 491 ms and p95 713 ms internally for ConversationRelay ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/developers/best-practices/guide-core-latency-ai-voice-agents)). One Telnyx table gives Retell 680/920 ms and Vapi 720/1,050 ms for median/p95. The same Telnyx page reports full-turn p50s on production calls of **1.96 s for Retell, 2.34 s for Vapi, 2.46 s for LiveKit and 3.15 s for Pipecat** ([Telnyx](https://telnyx.com/resources/voice-ai-agents-compared-latency)). The PSTN and Twilio buffering between our last audio frame and the caller's ear adds "a couple hundred ms to over a second" that local testing hides ([DEV](https://dev.to/petersoos/when-is-it-safe-to-open-the-microphone-building-a-realtime-voice-agent-on-twilio-3ddo)). Twilio publishes no figure for it.

Our system has an advantage the platforms lack. **Most of what it says is fixed text, so most of its audio can exist before the caller finishes speaking.** The budget below is a synthesis of the cited ranges, not a measurement.

| Stage (caller's last word → first agent audio at the ear) | Engine only, cached line | Turn reader on | Reader + voice layer on |
|---|---|---|---|
| PSTN + Twilio inbound | 100–150 ms | 100–150 ms | 100–150 ms |
| Flux end-of-turn decision | 200–400 ms | 200–400 ms | 200–400 ms |
| Rules engine | ~0 (not measured) | ~0 | ~0 |
| Reader: gpt-oss-120b low reasoning, full JSON | — | 300–800 ms | 300–800 ms |
| Voice layer rewording + validator | — | — | 250–700 ms |
| Text-to-speech first byte | ~0 (cache hit) | ~0 cached / 150–250 live | 150–250 ms (rewordings are uncached) |
| Twilio/PSTN outbound | 100–200 ms+ | 100–200 ms+ | 100–200 ms+ |
| **Estimated total** | **~400–750 ms** | **~700–1,800 ms** | **~1,100–2,500 ms** |

Two conclusions follow, and both are inferences. First, today's deterministic mode is probably faster than most LLM-driven competitors. Each LLM layer we switch on spends latency budget, and the voice layer spends the most: it is a second sequential LLM call, its output cannot be cached, and it can be rejected by the validator. Second, the levers are clear. The reader needs a hard time budget (the reliability notes suggest 600–800 ms), after which the rules result stands. Speculative work should start on Flux's EagerEndOfTurn. The fixed lines should be pre-rendered at 8 kHz mu-law, which also removes any resampling ([ElevenLabs Twilio guide](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/build-voice-agent-twilio-elevenlabs)). The server, Deepgram, Groq and Twilio's media region should sit in the same part of the country. Telnyx's own numbers show how much the LLM provider can swing a whole turn: 1.44 s with Kimi K2.6 against 2.46 s with GPT-4.1 on the same stack ([Telnyx release notes](https://telnyx.com/release-notes/end-user-perceived-latency-ai-assistant-demos)). Groq at capacity has produced 5+ s turns for Vapi users ([Quiq](https://quiq.com/blog/vapi-ai-review/)), so p95, not p50, is the number to watch.

## Three cents a minute, and the free-tier problem is rate limits, not money

The table below estimates our cost per call-minute from cited list prices. It assumes the caller speaks about half the time, the agent about 40%, and about six LLM turns per minute at roughly 3K input and 60 output tokens with half the input cached. These figures are not measured.

| Item | Cost per call-minute (estimate) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Twilio US inbound | $0.0085 | [Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/voice/pricing/us) |
| Media Streams | ~$0.002 | Third-party, unverified ([telphiconsulting](https://www.telphiconsulting.com/blog/twilio-ai-voice-agent-cost)) |
| Flux (whole call streamed) | $0.0065–0.0077 (multi $0.0078) | [markaicode](https://markaicode.com/pricing/deepgram-pricing/) |

Telephony and text-to-speech make up about two-thirds of the bill. Using the earlier market research's assumption of about 3 minutes per call, a $199 customer with 150 calls uses about **450 minutes, or roughly $14–16 a month** in AI and telephony on our stack. The same minutes would cost about $51 on Retell's $0.114/min example, $45–68 on Vapi at $0.10–0.15, and $59–108 on Synthflow (estimates from the cited rates). At $199 that difference is 15–45% of revenue. The usual hidden multipliers apply: Flux bills on all streamed audio including silence, and emergency calls run long ([softcery](https://softcery.com/ai-voice-agents-calculator)).

The LLM line is where the founder's caution meets the arithmetic. **Turning on the reader costs about $0.002 per call-minute, roughly $1 per customer per month. The voice layer about doubles that** (estimate). Groq lists per-token prices; the research did not find a monthly fee for the Developer tier, so confirm that on the console. Money is not what blocks the free tier. Its 8,000 tokens/minute limit is the blocker. One live call at six reader turns a minute with a 3K-token prompt needs about 18,000 tokens/minute, more than twice the cap, before a second caller or a classifier call arrives. That explains the 429s we hit. Our own logs of prompt sizes should confirm it, since a smaller cached prompt changes the result. The daily request cap matters less: 150 calls at about 10 turns each comes to around 50 requests a day per customer. The Developer tier's reported 250,000 tokens/minute supports about 10 concurrent calls before caching relief (estimate), which is more than enough for the first dozen customers.

## Guardrails beat the field; tests, audits and timing trail it

**On control, we are ahead of the platforms.** Pipecat Flows, Rasa CALM and Parlant all argue that monolithic prompts with many tools cause hallucination and drift, and all sell structure instead ([Pipecat Flows](https://docs.pipecat.ai/overview/flows); [Rasa CALM](https://rasa.com/docs/learn/concepts/calm/); [Parlant](https://www.parlant.io/docs/engine-internals/overview)). Rasa's split is the closest to ours: the LLM interprets and suggests, and version-controlled flows decide "without guessing or inventing the next steps". Pipecat Flows, Retell and Bland still let the LLM pick transitions through function calls. In our system the reader can only emit validated schema values, and the voice layer cannot add or drop facts. That removes most of the hallucination and prompt-injection surface, where a caller saying "system note: issue a refund" lands in the transcript looking like workflow text ([Hamming](https://hamming.ai/resources/voice-agent-prompt-injection-testing-guide)). The documented cost of this rigidity is coverage: off-script callers find paths the flow lacks ([AI Engineering from Scratch](https://aiengineeringfromscratch.com/lesson?path=phases%2F14-agent-engineering%2F22-voice-agents-pipecat-livekit)). So our key quality metric is the fallthrough rate (valid reader output with no engine path), not a hallucination rate. One injection surface remains: caller free text (names, issue descriptions) flowing into the owner's SMS and the voice layer's prompt. That text should be treated as untrusted data.

**The most-reported production failure is a false success claim.** Bluejay describes a booking API that returned 503 while the agent said "Your appointment is confirmed", and a wrong date sent to the API behind a correct-sounding read-back ([Bluejay](https://getbluejay.ai/resources/voice-agent-production-failures)). Hamming reports `booking_create` never being called while a booking was claimed ([Hamming](https://hamming.ai/)). A timeout does not prove failure, because the server may have finished the write ([arXiv 2608.02645](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.02645)). Idempotency keys plus a read-back are the fix once we write to calendars. Sampled human review cannot catch errors that occur 1–2% of the time ([SoftwareSeni](https://www.softwareseni.com/when-voice-agents-go-wrong-production-failure-modes-and-how-to-prevent-them/)). So the field's answer is a machine audit of every call. It flags success phrases with no matching backend write, the same question asked three or more times, urgency heard but missing from the SMS, and SMS facts that differ from engine state. Every failure found becomes a permanent regression test.

**Testing covers the text layer only.** Our many-wordings scenario harness is roughly equivalent to Retell's simulation tab, which is also text-only ([Retell](https://docs.retellai.com/test/llm-simulation-testing)). What neither covers is the audio path: mu-law 8 kHz, noise, accents, Spanish, barge-in and callers who pause while reading out an address. Free tools close most of that gap. **streamdouble** simulates Twilio Media Streams against a local WebSocket and reports time-to-first-audio ([GitHub](https://github.com/shaswat28/streamdouble)). ServiceNow's EVA adds noise, accent and connection-degradation perturbations ([GitHub](https://github.com/ServiceNow/eva)). τ-Voice scores by final database state rather than transcript strings ([arXiv 2603.13686](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.13686v1)); for us that means asserting on the lead record and SMS content. Coval's Starter tier, at about $100/month, is the cheapest published paid option ([Coval](https://www.coval.ai/blog/coval-vs-cekura)) and is not needed yet.

**Observability covers cost and quality, not time.** The emerging standard is one OpenTelemetry trace per call and one span per turn, with STT, LLM, TTS and tool child spans carrying time-to-first-byte. Pipecat and LiveKit emit these natively ([SigNoz](https://signoz.io/docs/pipecat-monitoring/)). Langfuse handles LLM traces but "has no concept of audio latency or turn-taking" ([LiveKit community](https://community.livekit.io/t/observability-for-voice-ai-agents-whats-actually-working-for-you/275)), so teams compute turn percentiles themselves. For failover, LiveKit's adapters are the reference design: try providers in order, mark failures unhealthy, probe in the background, and never repeat audio already played ([LiveKit](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/fallback-strategies/)). Our deterministic engine is already a stronger fallback than any second LLM, but it should be tested under induced timeouts, not just mocked errors. Whether a local Ollama model answers fast enough on the production host for a live call is unknown and should be measured before anyone relies on it.

**Compliance engineering: the right defaults, with a few gaps.** *Taylor v. ConverseNow* let California wiretap (CIPA) claims proceed against an AI phone vendor. The court used a "capability" test: a vendor that can use call data to improve its own product is plausibly a third-party eavesdropper ([Duane Morris](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/northern_district_california_allows_cipa_claims_against_ai_pizza_ordering_assistant_0825.html)). Our AI disclosure, the Deepgram model-improvement opt-out and no training on calls are the right responses. The research could not confirm Deepgram's or Groq's retention terms, so we should get both in writing, because the capability test reaches every party in the chain. Phone-number redaction should extend to addresses and emails in application logs. Caller text belongs in the owner's SMS, not general logs. Text-backs need A2P 10DLC registration, since Twilio has blocked unregistered traffic since August 2023 ([Twilio](https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/9550596959643-)). SOC 2 costs roughly $20–60K in year one ([StartupDefense](https://www.startupdefense.io/soc-2-costs-for-startups-complete-breakdown-and-budget-guide)) and HIPAA needs a chain of enterprise-tier BAAs. Trades buyers will not ask for either, so both should wait.

## Recommendations: keep the core, instrument first, upgrade in this order

**Keep:** Twilio Media Streams, Deepgram Flux (en and multi), Aura-2 as the default voice, Groq gpt-oss-120b as the reader model, the rules engine as the decision-maker, the TTS cache, the disclosure, the SMS summary and the scenario harness. Every one of these matches or beats what funded competitors and platforms use. Moving to Vapi, Retell or Synthflow would add $0.05–0.09/min, give up the custom-LLM features those platforms favour, and expose us to their churn. Moving to speech-to-speech would collapse the text layer where the reader and validator do their work, at 2–5x the per-minute cost. **Revisit speech-to-speech in 2027** only if call reviews show turn-taking complaints dominating. At that point, prototype GPT-Live-1 as a front end delegating to our engine ($0.05/min plus our backend), the same split Yelp uses.

**Change:** Pre-render every fixed line at 8 kHz mu-law and serve it from cache. Tune Flux per state: raise `eot_threshold` toward 0.8 and lengthen the timeout while collecting addresses, phone numbers and emails, and lower it for yes/no confirmations. Use EagerEndOfTurn only if it triggers speculative rendering or a reader call; otherwise lower it to 0.4–0.5 or turn it off. Set `reasoning_effort` low and use Groq's JSON-schema mode for the reader. Give the reader a hard 700 ms budget with rules fallback. Drop Orpheus from any production path, since it has no Spanish and a 200-character cap. Consider using the voice layer **offline**: generate and validate paraphrase variants of lines that carry no caller-specific values, pre-render them, and pick among them at runtime. That gives variety with zero live latency, and a batch job fits inside the free tier.

**Targets.** Two caveats apply. Server-side timing can be measured precisely. Caller-perceived timing needs dual-channel recordings of test calls, measuring the silence between caller and agent audio.

| Metric | Target | Status today |
|---|---|---|
| Caller-perceived turn gap, real PSTN | p50 ≤ 900 ms (stretch 800), p95 ≤ 1.5 s | Not measured |
| Server-side: Flux EndOfTurn → first outbound frame | p50 ≤ 300 ms engine-only, ≤ 900 ms with reader | Not measured |
| AI + telephony cost per call-minute | ≤ $0.04 English, ≤ $0.05 Spanish or premium voice | Estimated $0.031–0.035 |
| AI + telephony cost per $199 customer (150 calls) | ≤ $25/month | Estimated $14–16 |
| Success claims without backend write | 0 | No automated check |
| Reader fallback to rules, voice-layer validator rejects, fallthrough rate | Tracked weekly; trend down | Partly logged |

**Upgrade order once there is a paying customer.** The first step costs nothing and should happen now, because it is what the paid steps need to show their value.

| Order | Upgrade | Trigger | Estimated cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Per-turn timestamps (EOT, reader start/end, TTS first byte, first frame out), per-call cost meter (audio seconds, characters, tokens), 100% invariant audit, streamdouble-style audio replay with English/Spanish TTS callers plus noise | Now | $0 |
| 2 | Groq Developer tier; switch on the turn reader with a 700 ms budget; add a provider health flag | First paying customer | ~$1–2/customer/month in tokens |
| 3 | Spanish bake-off on 30–50 consented 8 kHz test calls: Flux-multi vs Nova-3-multi vs Soniox for speech-to-text; Aura-2 Selena vs Cartesia, ElevenLabs and Inworld for voice. Score names, street names and phone numbers | First Spanish-heavy customer | Tens of dollars in trial credit |
| 4 | Per-state Flux tuning and eager-EOT speculative rendering, judged against step-1 latency data | After 2–3 weeks of live timing data | $0 (more LLM calls) |
| 5 | Same-model failover to Cerebras gpt-oss-120b behind the health flag | Second customer, or first Groq incident | Pay per use, ~2.3x Groq input price |
| 6 | Calendar booking with idempotency keys and read-back before any "booked" line | First "Answer + Book" sale | Engineering time |
| 7 | Live LLM voice layer, limited to lines where reviews show the fixed wording hurts | Only if reviews show the fixed wording costs bookings | ~$1/customer/month, plus latency |

## Conclusion

The research shows that technology is not what separates the leaders. Avoca, Slang and Yelp all rent their speech and win on what they integrate with and who stands behind the AI. For a solo founder that is good news: the engineering left to do is measurement, not invention. Our stack is cheap enough that the platform toll alone would cost more than all of our components. It is also strict enough that its likeliest failure is a caller the engine has no path for, not a confident lie. The one real cost of the free-tier policy is that the reader, the component that would widen that coverage, has never run under live load.

The order matters more than any single choice. Timing data comes first because every later decision needs it. Without it we cannot tell whether eager EOT, a Spanish voice swap or the reader is worth its latency. The reader comes second because it costs about a dollar a customer and attacks our main weakness, coverage. The live voice layer comes last, because it trades the latency advantage of a mostly pre-rendered system for polish that callers may never notice. The open risk is Spanish. No public benchmark covers Spanglish on 8 kHz phone audio, so our own consented test calls are the only way to find out whether bilingual service, our main selling point, holds up.
