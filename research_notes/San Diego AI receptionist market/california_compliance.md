## 1. CIPA (Penal Code 631 / 632 / 637.2): consent, the "third-party eavesdropper" theory, real-time processing, SB 690

### Takeaway
CIPA is the biggest legal risk for this product. Since 2025, federal courts in California have let wiretap class actions go forward against AI call vendors (Google CCAI, ConverseNow, Otter.ai) under a "capability" test. Under that test, a vendor that merely *could* use call content for its own purposes, such as training models, can be a third-party eavesdropper even when the business hired it. SB 690, signed 2026-09-30, does **not** help: it only removes private suits under the pen-register provision (638.51) and leaves 631 and 632 fully in place.

### Cited Findings
**Capability test and the main AI-vendor cases**
- *Ambriz v. Google* (N.D. Cal., No. 3:23-cv-05437). Per trackers, in **February 2025** the court denied Google's motion to dismiss. It held that Google Cloud Contact Center AI is plausibly a "third party" under CIPA because it has the *capability* to use call data for its own purposes, such as training its models, even without proof that it did so. The test comes from *Javier v. Assurance IQ* (9th Cir. 2022). — [AI Lawsuit Tracker: Ambriz v. Google (May 2026 status)](https://ailawsuittracker.com/cases/ambriz-v-google-llc-3-23-cv-05437/); [Wilson Sonsini alert](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/us-federal-court-allows-cipa-class-action-against-ai-customer-service-provider-to-proceed.html)
- *Taylor v. ConverseNow Technologies* (N.D. Cal., No. 25-cv-00990-SI, Judge Illston). On **2025-08-11** the court denied the motion to dismiss on CIPA 631 and 632 claims. The plaintiff called Domino's, was routed to ConverseNow's AI voice assistant **without notice**, and gave PII. The court chose the "capability" test over the "extension" test. It held that ConverseNow's alleged capability to use caller communications to improve its products was enough at the pleading stage, even though ConverseNow had a contract with the restaurant. It also found that real-time interception was adequately alleged. — [Order (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-cand-3_25-cv-00990/pdf/USCOURTS-cand-3_25-cv-00990-0.pdf); [Duane Morris alert, Aug 2025](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/northern_district_california_allows_cipa_claims_against_ai_pizza_ordering_assistant_0825.html); [Squire Patton Boggs, Dec 2025](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2025/12/extra-large-pii-zza-courts-allows-california-privacy-class-action-to-proceed-for-use-of-ai-phone-call-assistant/); [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/conversenow-stuck-with-wiretap-lawsuit-over-ai-voice-assistant)
- *In re Otter.AI Privacy Litigation* (N.D. Cal., No. 5:25-cv-06911-EKL, Judge Eumi K. Lee). On **2026-08-13** the motion to dismiss was granted only in part. The federal Wiretap Act, CIPA and Illinois BIPA claims survive; computer-access claims were dismissed. The court reasoned that Otter allegedly **retains conversations and uses them to train its own models**, so it plausibly acts as an independent interceptor rather than a tool of the meeting host. This is a pleading-stage ruling only; no liability has been found. — [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/invited-participant-or-third-party-eavesdropper-court-holds-otterai-third-party); [Baird Holm](https://www.bairdholm.com/blog/core-privacy-claims-against-ai-note-taking-platform-survive-dismissal/); [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/when-ai-takes-notes-court-allows-privacy-claims-against-otterai-to-proceed/1834520); [Paul Hastings](https://www.paulhastings.com/insights/ph-privacy/ai-transcription-tools-when-a-robot-is-listening-courts-may-find-it-is-wiretapping)
- *Thompson v. SoundHound AI*, a California class action filed **2026-07-30**. It alleges that AI voice agents answering Chipotle's published phone numbers secretly routed callers through SoundHound's servers. No ruling had been found as of the search date. This comes from a vendor blog and was not checked against the docket. — [Basil AI article, 2026-09-11 (search summary only; fetch failed)](https://basilai.app/articles/2026-09-11-soundhound-chipotle-cipa-lawsuit-ai-voice-agents-phone-interception.html); [AI Lawsuit Tracker, agentic AI](https://ailawsuittracker.com/agentic-ai-lawsuits/)
- One source mentions a **May 2026** California ruling that CIPA reaches only telephone communications, not website software. That was a website-tracking case, not a voice case, and could not be checked against the order. It would not help a *phone* product anyway. — [AI Lawsuit Tracker](https://ailawsuittracker.com/agentic-ai-lawsuits/) (unverified)
- The 2024 *Patagonia/Talkdesk* case was not specifically confirmed in this session's searches (see Gaps).

**SB 690 (CIPA reform)**
- Governor Newsom signed SB 690 on **2026-09-30** (Chapter 976, Statutes of 2026). It takes effect **2027-01-01**. — [Morgan Lewis, Oct 2026](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/10/california-eliminates-private-right-of-action-under-cipas-pen-register-and-trap-and-trace-provision); [K&L Gates, 2026-10-08](https://www.klgates.com/thought-leadership/California-Narrows-CIPA-Litigation-SB-690-Ends-Pen-Register-and-Trap-and-Trace-Claims-10-8-2026); [WilmerHale, 2026-10-06](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20261006-cipa-reform-signed-into-law); [Alston & Bird](https://www.alston.com/en/insights/publications/2026/10/california-sb-690-enacted); [Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/client-advisories/reining-in-cipa-abuse-s-b-690-becomes-law-and-fixes-key-cipa-provision)
- SB 690 removes **private** suits over website, app and online tracking under the pen-register/trap-and-trace provision, **Penal Code 638.51**, leaving enforcement to the Attorney General. It does **not** amend the wiretap and eavesdropping sections **631 and 632**, which remain open to private plaintiffs. — [RecordingLaw summary](https://www.recordinglaw.com/news/california-sb-690-cipa-pen-register-carve-out/); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/10/california-eliminates-private-right-of-action-under-cipas-pen-register-and-trap-and-trace-provision)
- Reports say it applies retroactively to claims filed in the two years before it takes effect, which could reach cases filed on or after 2025-01-01. — [Search summary of firm alerts incl. Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/california-legislature-passes-sb-690-narrowing-private-rights-action-under)
- Note that some 2026 blog posts, such as Enzuzo's dated 2026-08-19, still described SB 690 as pending and are out of date. — [Enzuzo](https://www.enzuzo.com/blog/sb-690-and-cipa)

**Real-time processing without storage**
- In *ConverseNow*, the court found that **real-time** interception was adequately alleged. Under 631, liability turns on real-time interception or reading of contents "in transit", not on storage. — [ConverseNow order](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-cand-3_25-cv-00990/pdf/USCOURTS-cand-3_25-cv-00990-0.pdf)
- Commentators read *Otter* as turning on **what the vendor does with the recording** (retention and training for its own benefit), not on who invited the bot. — [Lawsuit Intelligencer](https://lawsuitintelligencer.com/otter-ai-cipa-ruling); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/invited-participant-or-third-party-eavesdropper-court-holds-otterai-third-party)
- A separate N.D. Cal. customer-service AI decision held that the vendor's **mere capability** to use the data was enough. — [UC Today summary](https://www.uctoday.com/security-compliance-risk/otter-ai-on-trial-and-the-ai-notetaker-industry-with-it/); [ailawsuittracker](https://ailawsuittracker.com/cases/ambriz-v-google-llc-3-23-cv-05437/)

### Inferences
- **Not storing audio does not by itself protect a vendor under 631.** Under the capability test, the question is whether the vendor *can* use content for its own purposes. Contract terms and architecture that genuinely bar secondary use (no training on customer calls, no cross-customer pooling, a narrow service-provider role) are the best structural defense. A clear **disclosure and consent at the start of the call** is the best practical defense, because consent defeats both 631 and 632. In ConverseNow the caller got no notice.
- A short opening line such as "This call is answered by an AI assistant for [Business] and may be recorded and transcribed. Stay on the line to continue" addresses CIPA consent and AI disclosure in one step. Staying on the line after notice is generally treated as implied consent, but a lawyer should confirm that for 632.
- The trend from 2025 to 2026 is plaintiff-friendly at the pleading stage, with new suits each year (Google CCAI in 2023, ConverseNow in 2025, Otter in 2025, SoundHound in July 2026). A solo vendor should expect that its *customers* (the small businesses) and the vendor could both be named defendants.

### Gaps
- Statutory damages under 637.2: the commonly cited figure is $5,000 per violation, or three times actual damages. This is background knowledge, **not verified in this session**, because leginfo fetches failed.
- No appellate (9th Circuit) ruling on the capability test as applied to AI voice vendors was found as of 2026-10-13. The Ambriz docket status after May 2026 is unknown.
- *Patagonia / Talkdesk* (2024) was not confirmed in this session's searches; check before citing.
- Whether a "no training, no secondary use" contract defeats the capability test has not been decided. Courts have so far ruled only on allegations.

## 2. AI disclosure laws: SB 1001, AB 2905, other 2025-26 California AI laws, FCC/FTC

### Takeaway
California has no clear statute that requires an *inbound* AI receptionist to say it is AI. AB 2905 (in force since 2025-01-01) covers AI voices in outbound telemarketing and robocalls, and whether it reaches inbound calls is unsettled. The FCC's February 2024 ruling covers AI voices under the TCPA for **outbound** calls. Its 2024 proposal to require AI disclosure inside calls was still not final as of September 2026. Even so, disclosing on every call is the safe and market-standard practice, and it supports CIPA consent.

### Cited Findings
- **AB 2905 (Low)** requires telemarketing calls that use AI-generated or significantly altered synthetic voices to disclose that use. The California AG's legal advisory places it in **Public Utilities Code 2874** (not the Civil Code). Effective **2025-01-01**. — [CA AG Legal Advisory on AI](https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/attachments/press-docs/Legal%20Advisory%20-%20Application%20of%20Existing%20CA%20Laws%20to%20Artificial%20Intelligence.pdf); [EM3 Law, 2024-10-30](https://blog.em3law.com/2024/10/30/ai-generated-robocalls/)
- The bill text expressly covers outbound automatic dialing-announcing devices. Whether it reaches **inbound** AI voice agents is "not expressly settled" by the text or any agency ruling as of mid-2026. — [Getaira compliance guide](https://www.getaira.io/resources/california-ai-voice-disclosure) (vendor blog)
- Some vendor blogs claim a $500-per-call CPUC fine under AB 2905. This could **not** be verified against statute text. — [Tradesly blog](https://www.tradesly.ai/blog/ai-voice-agent-compliance-california-ab-2905) (unverified claim)
- **FCC, February 2024 Declaratory Ruling.** AI-generated or voice-cloned voices are "artificial or prerecorded voice" under the TCPA, so outbound AI calls need prior express consent, and prior express **written** consent for telemarketing. — [Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/fcc-declares-tcpa-restrictions-apply-to-ai-generated-calls); [Squire Patton Boggs, Feb 2024](https://aihub.squirepattonboggs.com/2024/02/fcc-rules-voice-cloned-robocalls-are-covered-by-the-tcpa-as-artificial-pre-recorded/)
- **FCC NPRM FCC-24-84 (August 2024)** proposes defining an "AI-generated call", requiring disclosure inside the call, and requiring AI-specific consent language. As of **September 2026**, no Report and Order had been adopted, so it is not law. One source also says the FCC indicated that TCPA requirements do not extend to technology used to **answer inbound** calls. — [Evecalls summary](https://evecalls.com/labs/is-ai-cold-calling-illegal-how-to-comply-with-new-fcc-rules-and-global-regulations) (secondary; check the FCC docket)
- The TCPA mainly targets outbound calls, so its strictest consent and disclosure rules do not directly apply to an AI receptionist answering **inbound** calls. — [RealVoice compliance guide](https://www.realvoice.ai/blog/ai-receptionist-legal-compliance-guide) (vendor blog)
- The CA AG's advisory, "Application of Existing CA Laws to AI", says existing California consumer-protection, false-advertising and privacy laws apply to AI. — [CA AG Legal Advisory](https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/attachments/press-docs/Legal%20Advisory%20-%20Application%20of%20Existing%20CA%20Laws%20to%20Artificial%20Intelligence.pdf)
- Market practice: Smith.ai says its AI receptionist follows client disclosure preferences and **never claims to be human when asked directly**. — [Smith.ai AI receptionist page](https://smith.ai/ai-receptionist)
- One vendor blog reports high hang-up rates (it claims about 40%) after AI disclosure. This is a marketing claim with no method given. — [Tradesly](https://www.tradesly.ai/blog/ai-voice-agent-compliance-california-ab-2905) (unverified)

### Inferences
- For an inbound receptionist that does not sell anything outbound, no statute found here clearly *requires* AI disclosure on inbound calls in California. But three things make disclosure the prudent default: CIPA consent (section 1), the AG's position that UCL and false-advertising law apply to AI, and the risk that the FCC finalizes its 2024 proposal. **Never deny being AI when asked** (Smith.ai's standard). That is the minimum, and it lines up with SB 1001's anti-deception aim.
- If the product ever makes **outbound** AI calls (reminders, callbacks), AB 2905 and the FCC's 2024 ruling apply directly. That needs prior express consent (written consent for anything promotional) plus an AI disclosure.

### Gaps
- **SB 1001 (Bus. & Prof. Code 17940-17943)** text could not be fetched. From background knowledge, **not verified this session**: it makes it unlawful to use a bot to communicate with a person in California "online" with intent to mislead about its artificial identity in order to drive a sale or influence a vote, and clear disclosure is a safe harbor. Whether a phone call counts as "online" is debated. A lawyer should confirm.
- **SB 243** (companion chatbots, 2025) and the **CPPA ADMT regulations** were not specifically researched for voice receptionists. SB 243 appears aimed at companion bots, not business receptionists (unverified). ADMT duties attach to "significant decisions" (see section 3), which a receptionist taking messages and bookings likely does not make. That is an inference, not verified.
- No FTC rule specific to AI disclosure in calls was found. The FTC Act Section 5 deception standard applies generally (background, not sourced here).

## 3. CCPA/CPRA obligations: vendor as service provider; the small business; retention

### Takeaway
Most San Diego small businesses fall **below** the CCPA thresholds (more than about $26.6M revenue, or 100,000 or more consumers' data bought, sold or shared, or 50% or more of revenue from selling data), so CCPA often does not apply to the customer directly. The vendor should still offer a CCPA-grade service-provider addendum: larger customers need it, it supports the "no secondary use" CIPA defense, and it costs little.

### Cited Findings
- CCPA applies to for-profit businesses meeting one of these tests: gross revenue above **$26.625M** (inflation-adjusted; some sources still cite $25M), buying, selling or sharing personal information of **100,000 or more** consumers or households a year, or **50% or more** of revenue from selling or sharing personal information. — [Jackson Lewis FAQ, regs effective 2026-01-01](https://www.jacksonlewis.com/insights/navigating-california-consumer-privacy-act-30-essential-faqs-covered-businesses-including-clarifying-regulations-effective-1126); [Secure Privacy 2026 guide](https://secureprivacy.ai/blog/ccpa-requirements-2026-complete-compliance-guide)
- Service-provider contracts must bar using personal information outside the stated business purpose and combining it with other data. They must also cover help with consumer requests and opt-outs, flow-down to subcontractors, reasonable security, and compliance assistance. — [Modulos: CCPA ADMT service providers](https://docs.modulos.ai/frameworks/ccpa-admt/service-providers-and-admt-suppliers); [Sirion checklist](https://www.sirion.ai/library/contract-management/ccpa-compliance/)
- The CPPA finalized regulations on ADMT, cybersecurity audits and risk assessments. Some rules took effect **2026-01-01**, with ADMT duties phased in. Sources **conflict** on ADMT dates (2027-01-01 for significant-decision uses; pre-use notice, opt-out and access from 2027-04-01 per one source; 2027-12-31 for legacy processing per another). — [Wiley alert](https://www.wiley.law/alert-California-Finalizes-Pivotal-CCPA-Regulations-on-AI-Cyber-Audits-and-Risk-Governance); [Strategy Law, May 2026](https://www.strategylaw.com/blog/2026/may/californias-new-ccpa-rules-are-now-in-effect-is-/); [Modulos](https://docs.modulos.ai/frameworks/ccpa-admt/service-providers-and-admt-suppliers)
- Cybersecurity audits for smaller covered businesses (under $50M revenue) are first due **2030-04-01**. — [Secure Privacy](https://secureprivacy.ai/blog/ccpa-requirements-2026-complete-compliance-guide)
- Retention benchmark: Smith.ai keeps call recordings for **90 days**. — [Smith.ai docs: call recording](https://docs.smith.ai/article/fzv1b69n7t-call-recording-transcription-with-smith-ai)

### Inferences
- The vendor itself is unlikely to hit CCPA thresholds early on. But if any customer is covered, the vendor must act as a "service provider" under a compliant written contract, or it risks being a "third party" whose receipt of data counts as a sale or share. That label also hurts the vendor under the CIPA capability test.
- Practical retention policy: a short default for audio (for example 30-90 days, or none), longer for structured messages and leads, deletion on request, and no training on customer call data. These are design choices, not legal requirements found in sources.

### Gaps
- The exact ADMT effective dates and whether a receptionist's actions (booking or triage) count as a "significant decision" were not resolved. The final regulation text needs reading.
- CCPA's own retention-disclosure rule (privacy notice must state retention periods) is background knowledge, not verified here.

## 4. Texting: TCPA, A2P 10DLC, sole-proprietor brands, texting callers back, CTIA

### Takeaway
Texting the **business owner** (your own customer, who agreed in onboarding) is low risk. Texting the **caller** is the risk point. An inbound call alone may not be consent to texts, so the AI should get verbal permission during the call ("Can I text you a confirmation at this number?") and log it. All business SMS from 10-digit numbers must be on registered A2P 10DLC campaigns. Unregistered traffic on Twilio has been fully blocked since 2023-08-31.

### Cited Findings
- Twilio: messages from **unregistered** US 10DLC numbers have been fully blocked since the **2023-08-31** deadline (error 30034). — [Twilio Sole Proprietor FAQ](https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/9550596959643-A2P-10DLC-Sole-Proprietor-Brands-FAQ); [MessageDesk 2026 guide](https://www.messagedesk.com/blog/a2p-10dlc)
- **Sole-proprietor brand** limits: only for entities **without an EIN**, US or Canada only, **one campaign**, **one phone number**. Verification is by a real **mobile** number (VoIP and Twilio numbers fail), and throughput and volume are lower than standard brands. — [Twilio Sole Proprietor FAQ](https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/9550596959643-A2P-10DLC-Sole-Proprietor-Brands-FAQ); [Twilio: transition to Standard](https://www.twilio.com/docs/messaging/compliance/a2p-10dlc/transition-sole-proprietor-to-standard-brand)
- A secondhand figure puts sole-proprietor limits at 1 message per second and about 3,000 segments a day, with a 1,000-a-day T-Mobile sub-cap. Not confirmed in Twilio's current docs. — [Piyush Gambhir blog](https://www.piyushgambhir.com/blogs/twilio-a2p-brands-campaigns-costs-throughput-compliance) (unverified)
- Twilio recommends **Low-Volume Standard** brands for businesses with an EIN sending fewer than 6,000 segments a day. — [Twilio Sole Proprietor FAQ](https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/9550596959643-A2P-10DLC-Sole-Proprietor-Brands-FAQ)
- An inbound call may give consent for calls but **not necessarily for texts**. A missed call might imply consent to a callback but may not be adequate for a text unless the consumer was told about and agreed to that method. This is a secondary Q&A, not FCC authority. — [JustAnswer Q&A](https://www.justanswer.com/business-law/vzbcd-tcpa-consent-automated-text-after-missed-call.html)
- FCC 2024 revocation rules: consumers can revoke consent by any reasonable means. A single opt-out confirmation text with no marketing is allowed, and it is presumed within consent if sent within 5 minutes. — [FCC 24-24 Report and Order](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-24A1.pdf); [FCC fact sheet, 2024-01-25](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-400039A1.pdf); [Consumer Finance Monitor, 2024-02-19](https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2024/02/19/fcc-issues-final-rule-on-revocation-of-consent-for-robocalls-and-robotexts/)
- The FCC "one-to-one consent" rule for prior express written consent. — [FCC document DOC-408396](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-408396A1.pdf). Status should be confirmed: background knowledge, not checked this session, is that the rule was vacated by the 11th Circuit in January 2025.

### Inferences
- Architecture choice: with a vendor-owned Twilio account sending for many small businesses, each business generally needs its **own brand and campaign** (ISV model), or the vendor sends only to owners under its own brand. Registering each customer as a sole-proprietor brand only works for customers without an EIN, and each gets just one number.
- Caller texts should be **informational and transactional only** (confirmation, "the owner will call you back") with STOP handling, the business name, and no marketing unless there is written consent.

### Gaps
- **CTIA Messaging Principles and Best Practices** text was not fetched. Typical requirements (opt-in, HELP/STOP keywords, brand identification in the first message, no SHAFT content) are background knowledge only.
- No 2026-specific changes to 10DLC fees, vetting or timelines were found.
- No FCC ruling squarely on "text-back after an inbound or missed call" was found. A lawyer should confirm whether a single informational text the caller verbally agreed to needs anything more.

## 5. How competitors handle disclosure, recording consent and liability

### Takeaway
Every competitor whose terms surfaced (Goodcall, Rosie, Loman, plus the similar vendors Rossy and Smallest.ai) pushes recording-consent, TCPA and AI-disclosure duties onto the **customer** and makes the customer **indemnify** the vendor. Smith.ai requires the customer to accept terms before recording is turned on and never lets its AI claim to be human. Terms for Slang.ai, Podium and Avoca were not found.

### Cited Findings
- **Goodcall**: the user indemnifies and holds harmless the "Goodcall Parties" (including reasonable attorneys' fees) for claims from use of the Service and user content. Users are "solely responsible" for content, including recordings. No consent or AI-disclosure clause surfaced. — [Goodcall Terms of Service](https://help.goodcall.com/en/articles/8007566-terms-of-service)
- **Rosie**: the terms say Rosie records calls and creates transcripts used for quality monitoring, performance improvement and **training**. Its privacy policy makes the subscriber responsible for giving callers notice and getting any legally needed consent, and says callers consent by submitting information. — [Rosie Terms](https://heyrosie.com/legal/terms); [Rosie Privacy Policy](https://rosie.click/privacy.html) (search summaries; full fetch failed)
- **Loman AI**: the customer agrees to comply with the TCPA and state **two-party consent** recording laws, to obtain recording consents, and is "solely responsible" for the legality of recording. The customer indemnifies Loman for misuse. — [Loman Terms](https://loman.ai/terms-and-conditions) (search summary; full fetch failed)
- **Rossy AI** (comparable vendor): customer solely responsible for notice and consent before recording; vendor disclaims liability. — [Rossy Terms](https://rossy.ai/terms-of-service/)
- **Smallest.ai** (comparable voice-AI platform): customer solely responsible for recording consents **and** AI-voice disclosures under the TCPA and state law. — [Smallest.ai TCPA page](https://smallest.ai/tcpa-compliance)
- **Smith.ai**: the customer must expressly consent to terms before recording is enabled; recordings are kept 90 days; the AI is customizable to bar rules and disclosure preferences and never claims to be human when asked. — [Smith.ai docs](https://docs.smith.ai/article/fzv1b69n7t-call-recording-transcription-with-smith-ai); [Smith.ai AI receptionist](https://smith.ai/ai-receptionist); [Smith.ai call-recording best practices](https://smith.ai/blog/call-recording-best-practices)

### Inferences
- Rosie's terms permit using calls for **training**. Under *Ambriz*, *ConverseNow* and *Otter*, that is exactly the fact pattern that makes a vendor a plausible "third-party eavesdropper." A small vendor that commits to **no training on customer calls** gets both a legal edge and a sales edge.
- Pushing all compliance onto a plumber or dentist through indemnity is the industry norm. But a small customer cannot really fund an indemnity, and plaintiffs sue the vendor directly (ConverseNow, SoundHound, Google). Indemnity is no substitute for building the disclosure into the product by default.

### Gaps
- **Slang.ai, Podium, Avoca**: no terms of service, consent flow or disclosure script was found in this session.
- Actual on-call disclosure scripts used by each competitor (for example whether Goodcall or Rosie say "AI" in the greeting) were not found.
- Smith.ai's liability cap and indemnity terms were not found.

## 6. What a small vendor should put in its customer contract

### Takeaway
Combine three things. First, product defaults that create consent: an AI and recording notice at the start of every call that the customer cannot switch off for California numbers. Second, a CCPA service-provider addendum with a **no-training, no-secondary-use** promise. Third, the industry-standard allocation: the customer is responsible for its own disclosures, customizations and texting consent and indemnifies for those, while the vendor carries a mutual indemnity for its own breaches, capped. Have a California lawyer draft it.

### Cited Findings
- Compliance guides recommend contracts state whether the agency or the client is responsible for recording disclosures, plus indemnity for violations caused by client-provided scripts or configurations. — [Trillet voice-AI recording compliance](https://trillet.ai/blogs/voice-ai-call-recording-compliance) (vendor blog)
- Market examples of customer-side duties and indemnity: [Goodcall](https://help.goodcall.com/en/articles/8007566-terms-of-service), [Loman](https://loman.ai/terms-and-conditions), [Smallest.ai](https://smallest.ai/tcpa-compliance), [Rossy](https://rossy.ai/terms-of-service/)
- Required service-provider contract terms (purpose limits, no combining, help with consumer requests, subcontractor flow-down, security). — [Modulos](https://docs.modulos.ai/frameworks/ccpa-admt/service-providers-and-admt-suppliers)
- Recording consent and TCPA consent are separate legal duties, and California requires all parties to consent to recording. — [Captain Compliance](https://captaincompliance.com/education/ai-voice-calls-and-the-consent-gap-thats-sending-companies-to-court/)

### Inferences (suggested clause list; a lawyer must draft)
1. **Default disclosure**: the vendor plays an AI and recording notice at the start of each call. The customer may not remove it for calls from California or other all-party-consent states, and the customer bears liability if it overrides.
2. **Customer warranties**: accurate business information, lawful use, its own website and privacy-notice disclosures, consent for any marketing texts, and compliance with its own industry rules (for example HIPAA, so offer a BAA or exclude healthcare).
3. **Vendor data commitments**: CCPA service-provider terms, no training or model improvement on customer call content, no sale or sharing, a retention schedule, deletion on request, a subprocessor list (telephony, speech-to-text, LLM provider) with flow-down.
4. **Texting**: the customer authorizes 10DLC registration in its name, the vendor texts callers only for transactional purposes with recorded verbal consent, and STOP/HELP handling is built in.
5. **Indemnity**: the customer indemnifies for its content, configurations, overrides and marketing. The vendor indemnifies for its own breaches of data commitments and IP infringement. Mutual cap (for example 12 months of fees), with a carve-out discussion for data breach.
6. **Insurance**: consider tech E&O and cyber cover that does not exclude privacy and wiretap claims. Many policies exclude "wrongful collection" or BIPA/CIPA, so check.
7. **Arbitration and class waiver** in the vendor's contract binds only the business, not callers. It does not stop caller class actions.
